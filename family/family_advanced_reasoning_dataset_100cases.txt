Case 1: Posthumous Reproduction
[Academic Version]
This case examines posthumous reproduction, focusing on how kin roles are allocated and recognized
in practice. In Kyoto, Noah and Mina navigate a household where caregiving, legal status, and
belonging do not map neatly onto biological descent. The presence of Hana and Yara introduces
layered ties (biological, legal, and social), requiring negotiated rules for decision-making,
inheritance, naming, and obligations. Contemporary family systems emphasize intention and care as
determinants of kinship, while acknowledging lineage and cultural norms. Where institutions require
categorical answers (e.g., parent, guardian, heir), lived arrangements often remain fluid and
negotiated over time. This illustrates a broader move from fixed genealogical hierarchies to
relational, care-centered kinship, balancing autonomy with collective responsibility. Practically,
schools, clinics, and courts seek clarity on consent and authority, while households prioritize
trust and continuity of care. The case invites reasoning across biological, legal, and social
dimensions rather than any single definition of family.
